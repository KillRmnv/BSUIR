Coral reefs are among the most diverse and productive ecosystems on Earth, often referred to as the rainforests of the sea. They are built by tiny animals called coral polyps, which secrete calcium carbonate to form hard skeletons. Over thousands of years, these skeletons accumulate to create the massive structures we know as coral reefs. Reefs provide habitat for an estimated 25 percent of all marine species, despite covering less than one percent of the ocean floor. They support thousands of species of fish, invertebrates, plants, and microorganisms in complex food webs.

The health of coral reefs depends on a delicate symbiotic relationship between coral polyps and microscopic algae called zooxanthellae that live within the coral tissue. The algae photosynthesize and provide up to 90 percent of the coral's energy needs, while the coral provides the algae with shelter and nutrients. This partnership gives corals their vibrant colors. When environmental conditions become stressed, corals expel their zooxanthellae in a process called bleaching, which turns the coral white. If conditions do not improve, the coral can die from energy starvation.

Climate change poses the greatest threat to coral reefs worldwide. Rising ocean temperatures cause more frequent and severe bleaching events, giving corals less time to recover between episodes. Ocean acidification, caused by increased carbon dioxide absorption, reduces the ability of corals to build their calcium carbonate skeletons. Sea level rise, changing storm patterns, and increased UV radiation add additional stressors. Scientists estimate that 15 to 30 percent of the world's coral reefs have already been lost, and projections suggest that 70 to 90 percent could be lost if global temperatures rise by 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels.

Human activities beyond climate change also damage coral reefs. Overfishing disrupts the ecological balance of reef ecosystems, allowing algae to overgrow and smother corals. Coastal development causes sedimentation and pollution that degrade water quality. Destructive fishing practices like dynamite fishing and cyanide fishing directly destroy reef structures. Agricultural runoff introduces excess nutrients that promote algal blooms, reducing light availability for coral photosynthesis.

Conservation efforts are underway to protect and restore coral reefs around the world. Marine protected areas restrict human activities in sensitive reef zones, allowing ecosystems to recover. Coral restoration projects cultivate coral fragments in nurseries and transplant them onto degraded reefs. Research into heat-resistant coral strains and assisted evolution techniques aims to develop corals that can survive warmer ocean temperatures. International agreements like the Paris Agreement aim to limit global warming, which is essential for the long-term survival of coral reefs. Despite the challenges, the resilience and adaptability of coral ecosystems offer hope that with concerted conservation efforts, these vital ecosystems can be preserved for future generations.
